## 1 The idea: think global, act local

Some challenges are simply too big for one school, one company or one ministry. Climate change, planetary boundaries, the SDGs – these are **global** problems. But the actions that solve them happen **locally**: in a school building, in a workshop, in a municipality, in a company.

That is the idea of **"think global, act local"**: global challenges need action where people live and work. [CHECK: Juliana – origin of the phrase, if wanted]

The two levels need each other:

- **Global frameworks give direction.** The SDGs and the Paris Agreement show *where* the world is heading. The SDGs are a good framework to link any greening strategy, project or syllabus with them.
- **Local action makes it real.** A green campus, a greened curriculum, a partnership with a local company – these are where change becomes visible and measurable.

So the question is never "global *or* local". The question is: **which global goal does my local action serve – and which local action serves that goal?**

The idea links the two levels: global frameworks (SDGs, Paris) set the **direction**, while the **action** takes place locally – in a school, a company, a municipality. Waiting for global policy alone (option 1) does nothing, and blindly copying local solutions (option 3) ignores local conditions.

## 2 The whole-institution approach

Greening a VET institution is more than changing the curriculum. UNESCO-UNEVOC describes a **whole-institution approach**. Besides the curriculum, it includes:

- integrated **management and governance**,
- a sustainability **ethos** (the shared values of the institution),
- **community and stakeholder** engagement,
- **long-term planning**, and
- sustainability **monitoring and evaluation**.

All stakeholders – **leadership, teachers, learners, administration** – jointly develop a vision and a plan. Networks of institutions enable peer-to-peer learning.

UNEVOC describes **four steps**:

1. **Understanding** the process
2. **Planning** – an institutional greening plan with short-, medium- and long-term goals
3. **Implementing**
4. **Monitoring and assessment**

In TVET, the action can happen in five **action fields**: **green campus** (energy, water, waste, buildings), **green curriculum**, **green community** (partnerships with companies and the municipality), **green culture**, and **green research** – all of them fitting into the four steps above.

The four steps are a **cycle, not a one-off project**: understanding comes first (step 1), then the greening plan with its three time horizons (step 2), then the measures are put into practice (step 3), and finally progress is checked and the plan adjusted (step 4). After monitoring, the cycle usually starts again – that is what makes it a long-term approach.

## 4 From idea to action: the five micro-project steps

How do you turn an idea like Case C into a real project? The micro-project method gives you **five steps**:

1. **Profile** – name of your institution or department; field of work; core tasks.
2. **Assessment of the institution** – environmental and social practices; strengths, weaknesses, opportunities for sustainability.
3. **Defining project outcomes** – outcomes aligned with the SDGs / sustainability.
4. **Assessing resources** – which are relevant; which does your institution have, which does it need?
5. **Project plan** – a small-scale green initiative based on the defined outcomes.

Notice: steps 1 and 2 are *looking at yourself*, step 3 is *looking at the goals*, step 4 is *looking at what you have*, and step 5 is *doing*. Skipping an early step is the most common reason small green projects stall.

When you check a course or a workshop during step 2, three **lenses** help:

- **Resource efficiency** – material, energy, water; reuse versus replacement; waste,
- **Climate relevance** – emissions of the technology and the work process,
- **Occupational safety and health** – new hazards of green technologies.

**Check:** Complete the definition: Step 3 of the micro-project is defining project ______ that are aligned with the SDGs.

[[outcomes]]
****************************************
Step 3 is **defining project outcomes** – the results the project should deliver, aligned with the SDGs and sustainability. This keeps the project goal-oriented: step 5 (the plan) is then built *from* these outcomes, not the other way round.

## 5 Action canvas: plan your own small initiative

Now you do it yourself. The five prompts below follow the five micro-project steps. Answer each one in your learning journal – 3–5 sentences are enough. After all five, you have a simple **action canvas** for a small green initiative in your own environment.

> **Learning journal (step 1 – Profile):** Which institution or department would your small initiative belong to? What is its field of work and its core tasks?

<details>
<summary>Compare with an expert view</summary>

A good profile is short and concrete: the name of the institution or department, what it does, and who it serves. For a **ministry officer** that might be "the department for VET policy; core task is revising occupational standards". For a **school developer** it might be "curriculum support for about 20 VET schools". For a **principal** it might be "a VET school with mechanical and electrical programmes". Keep it to one or two sentences – the profile only orients the next steps.

</details>

> **Learning journal (step 2 – Assessment):** What are the environmental and social practices of that institution today? Name one strength, one weakness, and one opportunity for sustainability.

<details>
<summary>Compare with an expert view</summary>

Use the three lenses from page 4 to structure your answer: a *strength* could be "the workshop already reuses material" (resource efficiency); a *weakness* "energy use is not measured at all"; an *opportunity* "the electrical programme could teach PV systems" (climate relevance). One honest weakness is more useful than three vague strengths – step 5 builds on what you see clearly here.

</details>

> **Learning journal (step 3 – Outcomes):** What outcomes should your small initiative deliver, and which SDGs do they align with?

<details>
<summary>Compare with an expert view</summary>

Formulate outcomes as results, not activities: not "install solar panels", but "students can plan and maintain a PV system" or "the school measures its energy use and reduces it". Then name the SDGs the outcome aligns with – for energy and climate topics these are usually the goals about clean energy and climate action. If you cannot name an SDG, the outcome is probably too vague.

</details>

> **Learning journal (step 4 – Resources):** Which resources are relevant for your outcomes? Which does your institution already have, and which does it need?

<details>
<summary>Compare with an expert view</summary>

Resources are more than money. Think: people (a teacher, a caretaker, a company partner), knowledge, equipment, space, and time. A useful pattern is: "We **have** X and Y, we **need** Z – and Z can come from a local company partner or from a network with another school." Naming the gap now prevents the project from stalling in step 5.

</details>

> **Learning journal (step 5 – Project plan):** Write your small-scale green initiative in 5–8 sentences: what, who, when, and how you will check it.

<details>
<summary>Compare with an expert view – worked examples per role</summary>

Here are three **worked examples** (constructed, one per role) you can compare yours with:

- **Ministry officer (example):** "Within the revision of occupational standards for electricians, add one learning outcome on PV systems and energy efficiency (SDG 7, 13); test with two pilot schools; monitor with the school developers."
- **School developer (example):** "Set up a network of five VET schools that review one curriculum each with the three lenses (resource efficiency, climate relevance, OSH); share results in a peer meeting each term."
- **Principal (example):** "Name a sustainability team (teacher, student, caretaker, company partner); measure electricity and water use for three months; choose one green-campus measure and one curriculum project."

Notice the pattern in all three: a concrete first step, named actors, and a way to check progress. Download the action canvas in Moodle and mark it as done.

</details>

## What this means in Serbia

Serbia is already moving in this direction, and your work sits at the centre of it:

- The GIZ project ***Teaching skills for green transformation*** (Serbia, January 2024 – December 2026, partner: Ministry of Education) revises VET programmes to include green skills.
- Serbia's **Low Carbon Development Strategy** names green jobs in its 2050 vision.
- The **Roadmap for Circular Economy (2020)** invites industry, academia and civil society to act.

And the local opportunity is measurable: the Global Solar Atlas map below shows the **photovoltaic power potential across Serbia**.

That map turns the "example idea" from Case C into a grounded option: a VET school with an electrical programme could install a training PV system on its roof together with a local company. Green curriculum, green campus and green community – in one project, built on local data.

> **Learning journal:** Which of the three Serbian references (GIZ project, Low Carbon Development Strategy, Roadmap for Circular Economy) gives you the clearest entry point for your action canvas – and why?

<details>
<summary>Compare with an expert view</summary>

Most learners pick the **GIZ project**, because it is the one that directly touches their daily work: revised VET programmes and green skills are the shared object of ministry, school developers and principals. The **Low Carbon Development Strategy** is the strongest argument *upwards* – when you justify a project to a ministry, "green jobs in the 2050 vision" is a policy anchor. The **Roadmap for Circular Economy** is best for *partnerships*, because it explicitly invites industry, academia and civil society to act together. A strong answer names which of the three fits *your* role – ministry (strategy), school developer (project and partnerships), principal (programme and campus).

- [(X)] All stakeholders – leadership, teachers, learners and administration – jointly

UNEVOC is explicit: all stakeholders – **leadership, teachers, learners, administration** – jointly develop the vision and plan. A top-down plan (option 1) usually fails because the people who run the school daily are not part of it.

**2.** Which statements belong to the whole-institution approach? (Select all that apply.)

- [[X]] Integrated management and governance
- [[X]] A sustainability ethos
- [[X]] Community and stakeholder engagement
- [[ ]] Replacing the entire curriculum with a new one
- [[X]] Long-term planning with monitoring and evaluation

The approach covers **management and governance, ethos, community and stakeholder engagement, long-term planning, and monitoring and evaluation** – in addition to the curriculum. "Replacing the entire curriculum" is not part of the definition; greening works *within* the existing programmes, which is also why it is realistic for a VET school.

**5. (Scenario)** You are a principal and want to start the whole-institution approach. According to the UNEVOC four steps, what comes **first**?

- [( )] Writing the institutional greening plan
- [(X)] Getting all stakeholders to understand the greening process
- [( )] Starting with the green-campus measures
- [( )] Setting up monitoring and assessment
[[?]] The steps are: understanding, planning, implementing, monitoring.
****************************************
Step 1 is **understanding**: leadership, teachers, learners and administration must first understand the process – only then can they jointly develop the plan (step 2). Starting with measures (implementing) or monitoring before there is a shared understanding is the most common mistake in small green projects.

**6.** Why do the SDGs help in "think global, act local"?

- [( )] They replace local planning entirely
- [(X)] They are a framework to link any greening strategy, project or syllabus with global goals
- [( )] They are only relevant for ministries, not for schools
[[?]] Think about what the SDGs do for a *local* project, not what they do globally.
****************************************
The SDGs work as a **linking framework**: a local greening strategy, project or syllabus can be aligned with them, which shows *why* the local action matters globally. They do not replace local planning, and they apply at every level – including schools.
