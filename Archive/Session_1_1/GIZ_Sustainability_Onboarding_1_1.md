Part of: Session 1 – Introduction to Sustainability

## 1 What does “sustainability” mean in everyday language?

The word *sustainability* is used in two ways in everyday language. The Oxford dictionary gives two meanings of *sustainable*:

- using natural products and energy in a way that does not harm the environment,
- being able to continue, or be continued, for a long time.

The second meaning is easy to miss. In everyday German, *nachhaltig* is still used in the sense of “lasting, having a long-term effect” – for example a “nachhaltiger Erfolg” (a lasting success). That is very close to the modern idea of sustainability, and it shows that the word is older than the environmental debate.

## 2 The roots of the word

The modern German word *Nachhaltigkeit* goes back to the noun *Nachhalt*. It originally meant “support, backing” or “what is held back” – reserves kept for times of scarcity. It describes an old survival strategy of **precaution and self-limitation**: do not use everything today, keep something for a hard winter.

The adjective *nachhaltig* / *nachhalten* is documented in German by the end of the 18th century. [CHECK: Juliana – confirm the dating; Carlowitz used “nachaltende” already in 1713]

## 3 Carlowitz and the wood crisis

In the 18th century, German-speaking territories faced a severe **wood shortage**. Forests were over-used for timber trade, war, mining and smelting, tanning and glass-making. Forests shrank, and erosion and soil degradation threatened whole regions.

**Hans Carl von Carlowitz (1645–1714)** was the Saxon chief mining official (*Oberberghauptmann*) in Freiberg. In **1713** he published *Sylvicultura oeconomica* – the first systematic description of sustainable forestry.

Carlowitz also argued **economically**, not only ecologically: a country that destroys its forests destroys the basis of its own economy. For him, sustainability was a smart long-term strategy – not altruism.

## 4 Four core components of forest sustainability

Carlowitz' idea, developed further by **Georg Ludwig Hartig (1764–1837)** in his work of 1804, contains four core components. Hartig wrote that the present generation should use the forest as much as possible, but so “that the descendants can draw at least as much advantage from it” – an early form of **intergenerational justice** (fairness towards future generations).

| Component | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Long-term thinking** | The forest's services (timber, water storage, erosion protection, habitat) must be secured continuously; planning over decades to centuries. |
| **Social obligation** | Forests also serve public functions; public interests can limit private property rights. |
| **Economy** | Sustainability does not mean “no use”, but economic use with planned protection of the resource. |
| **Responsibility** | Awareness of the forest's importance for future generations. |

**Scenario:** You are a principal of a VET school. You have a limited budget for workshop equipment. One option: buy new machines every few years. Another option: buy fewer, high-quality machines now and budget for their long-term maintenance and gradual replacement. Which option fits the Carlowitz principle – and why?

<details>
<summary>Compare with an expert view</summary>

The second option fits the principle. Carlowitz' rule was to use the resource, but not to destroy the stock that produces it. In a school, the “stock” is the working capacity of the workshop: the machines, the trained staff who can maintain them, and the budget line that keeps them running. Buying new machines every few years spends the “capital” – after a few rounds, there is nothing left to maintain. Maintaining and gradually replacing a smaller set of good machines keeps the stock intact and lets the school “live from the interest”. A ministry officer might frame it the same way: fund maintenance lines, not only new purchases.

</details>

## 5 “Live from the interest, not from the capital”

The forest idea is often explained with a money metaphor. A forest is managed sustainably if the annual harvest equals the annual growth – the stock (the “capital”) stays intact, and only the growth (the “interest”) is used.

> “Live from the interest, not from the capital.”

For a long time, this principle stayed in specialised fields such as forestry and resource management. Only in the second half of the 20th century did it become a global idea. The Brundtland Report (1987) put *sustainable development* at the centre of the international debate (details in Session 1_2). It defined:

> “Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.”
>
> -- World Commission on Environment and Development (WCED, 1987), *Our Common Future*

**Check:** Complete the definition: development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future ______ to meet their own needs.

[[generations]]
****************************************
The missing word is **generations**. The definition is from the WCED (1987): it connects the needs of people living today with the ability of **future generations** to meet their own needs.

A school developer might name company partnerships: each partnership takes years to build and is lost quickly if it is not maintained. A principal might name the school's reputation for quality, or the workshop buildings themselves. All three are “stocks” in the Carlowitz sense: they produce value year after year, but only if they are kept intact. If you can name one such stock in your own area of responsibility, you have understood the core idea.

## What this means in Serbia

Forestry is still a sustainability arena in Serbia today. Serbia's *Strategy for Low Carbon Development 2023–2030 with projections until 2050* (Official Gazette No. 46/2023) covers energy, industry, transport, agriculture, **forestry** and waste – a reminder that the original “forest” of sustainability is still part of national planning.

The Serbian word for sustainability is *održivost*, from *održati* – to keep, to maintain. [CHECK: Juliana / GIZ Serbia – confirm and add one sentence on how the term is used in Serbian VET documents]

**Transfer:** Which “resources” of a VET system must be kept like Carlowitz' forest – for example qualified teachers, company partnerships, school buildings? Name one and say what “sustained use” would look like for it.

<details>
<summary>Compare with an expert view</summary>

For qualified teachers, “sustained use” means planning replacement and continuing education so the stock of experienced teachers does not shrink below what schools need. For company partnerships, it means maintaining the relationship – joint projects, clear communication – instead of activating it only when a new programme is launched. For school buildings, it means a maintenance budget that keeps the stock usable for decades. In all three cases, the Carlowitz rule applies: use the resource, but keep the stock that produces it.

</details>
