Part of: Session 1 – Introduction to Sustainability.

Sustainability is often treated as a modern idea. It is not. It starts as a practical rule for one trade – forestry – and ends up as the guiding goal of the whole global community. This session walks you through that story, one milestone at a time.

## 1 Where the idea starts: forestry, 1713

The story begins in 1713. Hans Carl von Carlowitz published *Sylvicultura oeconomica*, a book on the management of forests. He used the German term *Nachhaltigkeit* for a simple rule of **sustained-yield forestry**: cut only as much wood as can grow back.

This was not a global vision. It was a rule to keep one valuable resource – the forest – productive for the next generation of foresters.

## 2 The 1970s: awareness and the first global conference

Two things changed the debate in the early 1970s.

In 1962, Rachel Carson published *Silent Spring*. She warned about pesticides and pollution and showed that local pollution can have global consequences. The book raised environmental awareness worldwide.

In 1972, the UN held its first global environment conference in **Stockholm**: 114 states, about 1,200 delegates including many NGOs. They agreed a declaration of principles and an action plan, and the **UN Environment Programme (UNEP)** was founded in Nairobi. UNEP is the UN body for the environment.

Also in 1972, the Club of Rome published *The Limits to Growth*. It presented a computer model from MIT that showed "overshoot and collapse" when growth meets physical limits. (You will examine this report in Session 5_1.)

## 3 1987: the Brundtland definition

In 1987, the World Commission on Environment and Development – chaired by Norway's Prime Minister Gro Harlem Brundtland – published the report *Our Common Future*.

The report put **sustainable development** at the centre. For the first time, it explicitly linked economic development and environmental protection. And it gave the definition that is still the reference point today:

> "development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs"
> — WCED (1987)

The definition combines three elements: the **needs of the present**, **justice towards future generations**, and the **limits of natural systems**.

<details>
<summary>Compare with an expert view</summary>

A ministry officer often points to **present needs**: education is a basic need, and the system must work for the people living today. A school developer often points to **future generations**: the programmes we design now shape the workforce of the next 30–40 years. A principal often points to the **limits of natural systems**: a school is a physical building that consumes energy and space. All three readings are valid – the definition is deliberately open, and that is why it still works.

</details>

## 4 1992: Rio turns the idea into a global programme

In 1992, the UN Conference on Environment and Development (UNCED, the "Earth Summit") met in Rio de Janeiro. It produced four key results:

- **Agenda 21** – an action programme with 40 chapters, for governments, business, NGOs and citizens at local, national and global level.
- **Rio Declaration** – 27 principles, including the **polluter-pays principle** (the one who causes pollution pays for it) and the **precautionary principle** (act before harm is fully proven).
- **UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC)** – the treaty that later led to the Kyoto Protocol (1997).
- **Convention on Biological Diversity** – protection of the variety of life on Earth.

Rio turned sustainable development from a scientific and political concept into a **global guiding principle**. Agenda 21 became the template for many local, regional and national sustainability processes.

## 5 2015: measurable goals and the Paris temperature limit

In 2000, the UN adopted the **Millennium Development Goals (MDGs)**: 8 goals for 2000–2015 on poverty, education, gender equality, health and environmental sustainability – the first attempt to make development measurable with time-bound goals.

In 2015, two things happened. First, **Agenda 2030** with the **17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)** was adopted: 169 targets for all UN member states, valid 2016–2030. Second, at climate conference COP21 in Paris, the **Paris Agreement** was adopted: limit warming to well below 2 °C above pre-industrial levels, preferably 1.5 °C.

## 6 What this means in Serbia

The timeline does not stop in 2015. The Western Balkans and Serbia added their own entries:

- **Sofia Declaration on the Green Agenda for the Western Balkans** – endorsed 10 November 2020 at the Sofia Summit; Western Balkan leaders commit to the green agenda in line with the EU Green Deal (the EU roadmap to become the first climate-neutral continent by 2050, adopted in 2019).
- **Serbia: Law on Climate Change** – adopted 18 March 2021; it requires a low-carbon development strategy with an action plan and monitoring, reporting and verification (MRV) of emissions.
- **Strategy for Low Carbon Development 2023–2030** (Official Gazette 46/2023) – its 2050 vision includes **green jobs**.
- **GIZ project "Teaching skills for green transformation"** – January 2024 to December 2026, with the Serbian Ministry of Education.

> **Learning journal:** Place the Sofia Declaration (2020) and Serbia's Law on Climate Change (2021) on the timeline you have built. What changed for VET in Serbia because of these steps?

<details>
<summary>Compare with an expert view</summary>

A ministry officer would note that green transformation is now **legally anchored** in Serbia: the Law on Climate Change requires a low-carbon development strategy with monitoring of emissions, so schools and training systems are part of that framework. A school developer would point to the **Strategy for Low Carbon Development 2023–2030**: its 2050 vision explicitly includes green jobs, which means the occupations your schools train for are changing. A principal would add that the GIZ project with the Ministry of Education (2024–2026) is one concrete response – it builds the skills that the strategy assumes. None of this is "imported": it sits on the same timeline as Brundtland and Paris.

</details>

**Scenario (ministry officer):** You are a ministry officer preparing a short text that justifies green skills in new occupational standards. Which milestone would you quote, and why?

> **Learning journal:** Write the two or three sentences you would use.

<details>
<summary>Compare with an expert view</summary>

A strong answer quotes the **Brundtland definition (1987)** to show that development and environment belong together, then the **Paris Agreement (2015)** and **Agenda 2030** to show that the goals are concrete and time-bound, and finally Serbia's **Law on Climate Change (2021)** to show that the commitment is national and legal. The logic: the global definition gives the "why", the 2015 agreements give the "what", and the Serbian law gives the "here and now". A principal reading the same text would check whether the standard names the occupations where green skills are needed – that is the step from milestone to classroom.
