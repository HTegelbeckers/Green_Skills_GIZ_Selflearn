## 1 Climate vs. weather – and two more terms

Start with the basics, because the debate is easier once the terms are clear.

- **Weather** is what the atmosphere does on a given day: rain, heat, wind.
- **Climate** is the summary of weather phenomena over time – the state of the atmosphere and the interaction of all components of the Earth–atmosphere–ocean system.
- **Climate change** is the phenomenon of constant and extreme changes of the climate.
- **Global warming** is the long-term heating of Earth's surface. In 1990, warming was between 0.3 and 0.6 °C; today it is close to 1.3 °C (state 2024). Human-made climate change is scientifically proven.

NASA explains the difference between the two most common terms in under two minutes. Watch for: what NASA says "global warming" and "climate change" each mean, and which term is the broader one.

## 2 The evidence: how warm is "warm"?

The scientific community summarises its findings in regular assessment reports. The IPCC Synthesis Report (2023) lists seven key points:

1. Warming is about 1.1 °C.
2. Extreme weather hits people where they are most vulnerable – in the Global South.
3. Climate change is becoming the most important cause of species extinction.
4. 1.5 °C is the limit that decides about life – and the world is about to fail here.
5. Emissions must fall faster.
6. Climate protection costs – but the climate crisis costs more.
7. **A liveable future is still possible.**

The IPCC explains these key messages in a short video. Watch for: what the report says about the 1.5 °C limit, and which point gives hope.

!?[IPCC: AR6 Synthesis Report – Climate Change 2023](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YIFCSZYU2LM "IPCC – AR6 Synthesis Report: Climate Change 2023 (9:58)")

The picture below is called **warming stripes**: each band is one year, from 1850 to 2018. The left side is cool, the right side is hot – you can see the shift with your own eyes.

The numbers get sharper year by year. Copernicus reports that **2024 was the warmest year on record**, about **1.60 °C** above the pre-industrial level (1850–1900) – the first calendar year above 1.5 °C. Each of the ten years from 2015 to 2024 was among the ten warmest on record. March 2025 was the second-warmest March globally, and April 2024 – March 2025 was 1.59 °C above pre-industrial.

## 3 Why wording matters

Now to the heart of this session: the same facts can be told with different words.

- "Climate change" sounds **neutral and slow**.
- "Climate **crisis**", "climate **emergency**" or "climate **breakdown**" express **urgency** – they say: we need to act now.

In May 2019, the newspaper *The Guardian* changed its style guide to prefer "climate emergency, crisis or breakdown" over "climate change" – and "global heating" over "global warming" – to describe the situation more accurately.

BBC Learning English shows how people actually use the phrase "climate emergency". Watch for: when people use the phrase, and what tone it has in everyday conversation.

!?[BBC Learning English: Climate emergency – The English We Speak](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3SOENeBwJyo "BBC Learning English – Climate emergency – The English We Speak (2:39)")

There is a fair critique of crisis language: it can create fear or fatigue, and people may stop listening. The balance used in this training is: **name the problem honestly – and show that a liveable future is still possible** (that is IPCC key point 7).

Words also shape who feels responsible. **Climate justice** points to who causes and who suffers: the Global North versus the Global South, and the fact that women are more affected by extreme weather.

**Climate change** is the neutral, scientific term for long-term shifts in climate patterns. **Climate crisis** and **global heating** are urgent terms: the crisis demands immediate action, and "heating" stresses the human cause and speed. **Climate justice** is normative – it is about the fair sharing of burdens and responsibilities between people and regions.

## 4 Choosing your words

Let's train the vocabulary you will need in your own documents and meetings.

**Check:** Choose the right word: Climate [[ (change) | crisis ]] describes long-term shifts in a neutral way; climate [[ change | (crisis) ]] stresses the need to act now.
****************************************
"Climate **change**" is the neutral, scientific description of long-term shifts. "Climate **crisis**" adds urgency: it frames the situation as one that demands immediate action. Both are correct – they simply do different jobs in a sentence.

## 5 A scenario: the introduction of a new curriculum framework

Imagine you are a ministry officer writing the introduction of a new curriculum framework for VET schools. A colleague says: "Let's write 'climate change' – it sounds neutral and safe." Another says: "Write 'climate crisis' – we need to show that action is urgent."

Which word do you choose for the official introduction – and how do you handle the colleague who wants the other one? Think in three or four sentences. There is no single right answer; a good answer gives a reason.

A good explanation sounds like this: "Climate change is the neutral, scientific term – it describes constant and extreme changes of the climate, and it is proven that humans cause it. Climate crisis adds urgency: it says the situation demands action now. In a ministry document, 'climate change' keeps the text factual; 'climate crisis' signals that this is a priority. A strong introduction can use both – name the change first, then explain why it is a crisis." A school developer might add: the pair of words also decides how strongly you push a topic in teacher training. A principal might prefer "crisis" in an internal letter to mobilise staff. All three perspectives are valid – the wording sets the tone of the whole text.

## 6 What this means in Serbia

In May 2014, floods hit Serbia. More than **1.6 million people** were affected – about 22 % of the population. **57 lives were lost** (World Bank figure; other sources give 51–60). About 32,000 families were forced from their homes. Total damage and losses were estimated at **more than EUR 1 billion** – about 4–5 % of GDP – and recovery needs were assessed at about USD 1.5 billion by the Government together with the EU, the UN and the World Bank (June–July 2014). The ILO estimated about **51,800 jobs temporarily lost**; energy and agriculture were among the hardest-hit sectors.

Obrenovac, near Belgrade, was one of the worst-affected towns:

![Flooded streets and houses in Obrenovac, Serbia, 2014](img/1280px-Flood_in_Obrenovac__2014.jpg "Gmihail at Serbian Wikipedia – CC BY-SA 3.0 RS")

*Image: Flood in Obrenovac, 2014 – Gmihail at Serbian Wikipedia – CC BY-SA 3.0 RS – via Wikimedia Commons – unchanged*

[CHECK: Juliana – the floods cannot be attributed to climate change one-to-one; keep the wording careful]

So the honest question is not "climate change or climate crisis?" but: **for the people in Obrenovac in May 2014, was this "climate change" or a "crisis"?** For them, it was clearly a crisis – homes, jobs and livelihoods were at stake, in energy and agriculture above all. That is what the word "crisis" is for.

> **Learning journal:** How did the 2014 floods affect schools or vocational training in your region? What do you still see from them today?

<details>
<summary>Compare with an expert view</summary>

Answers vary by region, but three perspectives are typical. A ministry officer might point to the recovery funding: after a loss of more than EUR 1 billion and about 51,800 temporarily lost jobs, rebuilding schools and training programmes was part of the recovery needs. A school developer might note that flood protection and emergency preparedness are now topics in school development plans, and that teacher training includes how to support students who lost their homes. A principal in a town like Obrenovac might simply say: the campus was damaged, some students moved away, and the school had to keep working under very difficult conditions. A good answer names one concrete effect and one thing that is still relevant today.
