## 1 A report from 1972

In 1972, a team at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) – Donella Meadows, Dennis Meadows and colleagues – wrote a report for the **Club of Rome**: *The Limits to Growth*. The Club of Rome is a group of thinkers, scientists and business people that commissions studies on global problems. Its co-founder Aurelio Peccei explained the club's purpose in an archive film.

The report used a computer model called **World3**. It worked in the tradition of Jay Forrester's **system dynamics** – a way of modelling how parts of a system influence each other over time. World3 simulated how population, industrial production, food, resources and pollution interact over time.

One important point: the report is a **scenario** study, not a precise forecast. A scenario shows what *could* happen under certain assumptions. Different assumptions lead to different futures – including stable ones.

In this short video, Dennis Meadows, one of the report's authors, explains how the study came about. Watch for: who commissioned the report, and what the team wanted to show about the relationship between growth and the planet.

## 2 The core message: overshoot and collapse

The core message of the report: if growth in population and production continues on a **finite planet** – a planet with limited space and resources – the system will reach its limits. The typical pattern in the model is **"overshoot and collapse"**: the system grows beyond what it can support, and then breaks down – unless societies change course early.

## 3 Why the debate still matters

The report is 50 years old – so why discuss it now? Because the basic problem is still there:

- **Ecological footprint:** humanity uses resources as if we had **1.71 Earths** – we consume more than one planet can provide.
- **Planetary boundaries:** seven of the nine planetary boundaries have been crossed (2025) – more in Session 3_2.
- **The SDGs:** they do not fundamentally question the growth and consumption model, although that model is a main driver of environmental degradation. You can see the tension between SDG 8 (growth) and SDG 13 (climate).
- **Criticism of the green economy:** it ignores the need to reduce consumption, sticks to growth – "more of the same".
- **Rebound effect:** efficiency gains can be offset when people use more of the cheaper product or service. For example: a more efficient car may lead to more driving.

## 4 Three responses to limits

The responses to ecological limits can be grouped in three:

- **Efficiency** (weak sustainability): produce the same result with less input – less energy, less material, less waste.
- **Sufficiency**: use less in total. The question is not "how cheap can we make it?" but "do we need it at all?"
- **Degrowth strategies** (strong sustainability): plan an economy that works within ecological limits, not just a greener version of today's economy. "Degrowth" here means planning for less total growth – not "no economy at all".

Remember the **rebound effect**: if efficiency makes something cheaper, people may use more of it – and the saving disappears. That is why sufficiency is often discussed together with efficiency.

## 5 What this means in Serbia

Limits to growth are not only a global debate. Serbia has a concrete example: **lignite** is a finite, non-renewable resource.

- The Kolubara basin used to produce **28–30 million tonnes** of lignite per year.
- This fell to **25 million tonnes in 2022** and to **22 million tonnes in 2023 and 2024**.
- About **60 %** of Serbia's electricity still came from lignite in 2024.

![Nikola Tesla thermal power plant in Obrenovac, Serbia](img/1280px-Urovci__Termoelektrana_Nikola_Tesla__12.JPG "Mickey Mystique – CC BY-SA 3.0")

*Image: Urovci, Termoelektrana Nikola Tesla – Mickey Mystique – CC BY-SA 3.0 – via Wikimedia Commons – unchanged*

Serbia's **Low Carbon Development Strategy** (2023–2030, with 2050 projections) plans the shift of energy, industry, transport, agriculture, forestry and waste – and names **green jobs** in its 2050 vision.

The transfer question for VET: which programmes depend on the growth of a finite resource (mining, lignite power), and which could grow within limits (renewables installation, efficiency, repair)?

**Scenario (ministry officer):** A VET school near the Kolubara basin reports that enrolment in its mining-related programme is falling. As a ministry officer responsible for VET planning, which response fits the logic of "limits to growth" best?

- [( )] Keep funding the mining programme exactly as before – the resource will surely come back.
- [(X)] Plan the next five years for both: support the transition of existing mining programmes and build programmes that can grow within limits, such as renewables installation, efficiency or repair.
- [( )] Close the school immediately, because a finite resource means the sector is over.
[[?]] A finite resource means the old sector will shrink – but the skills of the region still need a future.
****************************************
The logic of limits to growth says that a sector built on a finite resource will shrink – but that does not mean the region has no future. Planning for **both** the transition of existing programmes and the growth of programmes that work within limits (renewables installation, efficiency, repair) is the balanced response. Keeping everything as before ignores the limit; closing the school ignores the skills and jobs the region still needs.
****************************************

A ministry officer might choose **energy**: if about 60 % of electricity still comes from a finite resource, VET planning must prepare both the transition away from lignite and the growth of green jobs named in the Low Carbon Development Strategy. A school developer might choose **mechanical engineering**: the sector does not disappear, but the skills shift towards efficiency, repair and maintenance of new technologies. A principal might choose **construction**: demand may move from new, resource-heavy projects to renovation and energy efficiency. In all three cases, the key question is the same: does the sector depend on the growth of a finite resource, or can it grow within limits?
