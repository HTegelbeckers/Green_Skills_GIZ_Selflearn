## Why this topic matters

Sessions 1 to 5 showed you the challenges: ecological limits, social inequality, economic pressure. This is the last topic of the course, so it asks a different question: **what can we actually do about it?**

The answer starts with three levels of action:

- **Individuals** – everyday decisions: food, transport, energy use, travel; participation and social engagement.
- **Organisations** – work decisions: changing how a school, a campus or a company runs. In a TVET campus, this can look like appointing a **sustainability officer** who handles strategy, networking, climate protection management and an action plan for Education for Sustainable Development (ESD).
- **Societies** – democracy and politics: policy frameworks such as the Paris Agreement, the EU Green Deal, the Green Agenda for the Western Balkans and Serbia's climate law.

Two more terms will help you think about action. A **footprint** is the negative, passive side of our impact on the world. A **handprint** is the positive, active side. Education and empowerment together create a **brainprint**. Responsibility for the transformation lies with society, science, policy makers and industries.

One important idea frames the whole topic: the **great transformation**. The German Advisory Council on Global Change (WBGU) called for a "social contract for sustainability" in 2011. The transformation is not only a technical task – it is a shared, political decision.

The video below makes the point in a very direct way. Watch for: what individuals can do – and what only systems (politics, companies) can change.

## The big picture

So how does a great transformation look in practice? In a VET school, it is not one project. It is a set of action fields. Juliana Hilf names five:

- **green campus** – the buildings, energy and everyday life of the school,
- **green curriculum** – what students learn,
- **green community** – the school's role in its neighbourhood and region,
- **green culture** – how sustainability is lived, not just taught,
- **green research** – the school's own learning and innovation.

A solar installation technician on a rooftop is a small, concrete example of what "green campus" and "green curriculum" can look like together: a real skill, a real job, a real contribution to the energy transition.

The action fields are where a school can actually build a handprint.

Before you go on, keep Juliana's five take-home messages in mind. They are the thread that runs through the rest of this course:

1. Keep reminding yourself of the necessity and power of (Education for) Sustainable Development.
2. Green empowerment and competences matter more than just content.
3. Be a change maker and let your colleagues, friends and students become change makers.
4. Schools and TVET institutions play the role of a catalyst for a sustainable economy, ecology and social equality.
5. Network with others – a team is stronger than an individual.

## Learning journal

> **Learning journal:** Write down one concrete **handprint** idea for your own institution – a ministry, a school or a campus. It does not have to be big. It does not have to be funded. It only has to be something you could start.

<details>
<summary>Compare with an expert view</summary>

A good handprint idea is small enough to start and specific enough to name. A ministry officer might think: "We can add one green-skills indicator to the next curriculum review." A school developer might think: "I can offer one half-day workshop on green campus to the schools I support." A principal might think: "We can start a monthly energy-use check with our facilities team." Notice what all three have in common: they are not about changing the whole system. They are about adding one concrete, visible step. That is how a handprint starts – and it is the seed of the action plan you will build in Session 6_3.

</details>

## What this means in Serbia

Serbia's Low Carbon Development Strategy names **green jobs** in its 2050 vision. The GIZ project *Teaching skills for green transformation* (2024–2026) is revising VET programmes to include green skills. In other words: the system is already moving. Your role – steering the VET system, supporting schools or leading a school – is one of the places where the three levels of action meet.

> **Learning journal:** Which of the three levels – individual, organisation, society – do you have the most direct influence on in your current role? Name one concrete step you could take there.

<details>
<summary>Compare with an expert view</summary>

Most ministry officers will say **society**, because they shape policy, standards and funding. Most school developers will say **organisation**, because they work directly with schools and teachers. Most principals will say **organisation** too, but at a different scale: they decide what happens inside their own campus. None of these answers is wrong. What matters is naming one step you can actually take – not the whole transformation. The three levels are not a hierarchy of importance; they are three places where the same transformation happens.

</details>
