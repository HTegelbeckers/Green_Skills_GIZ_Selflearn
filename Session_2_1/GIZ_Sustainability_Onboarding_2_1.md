Part of: Session 2 – Basic Concepts and Policies

## 1 What do we mean by "sustainability"?

The word is used in many ways. Before you can discuss sustainability in policy or in school development, it helps to know which definitions exist. Here are five, from everyday language to education policy:

| Definition | What it stresses |
| --- | --- |
| **Oxford dictionary** | (a) using natural products and energy in a way that does not harm the environment; (b) the ability to continue for a long time |
| **Brundtland / WCED (1987)** | "Sustainable development is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs." |
| **"Enough, for everyone, forever."** | A short African paraphrase of the Brundtland idea, used in GIZ trainings |
| **Carlowitz (1713)** | Use the forest so that there is a "continuous, steady and sustained use" – cut only what grows back |
| **UNESCO – Education for Sustainable Development (2022)** | "ESD enables all people to understand the impact of their own actions on the world and to make responsible, sustainable choices." |

You can see the family resemblance: most definitions combine **environment**, **time** (forever, future generations) and **people** (everyone). But they stress different parts.

## 2 Why sustainability is normative

Now the key idea of this session. The concept of sustainable development is **not the result of scientific research**. As Juliana Hilf puts it: "The concept of sustainable development is not the result of scientific research; instead, it is an ethically grounded concept."

What does **normative** mean? It means: the concept says how society **should** develop. It is a social and political guideline, not a technical measurement. And because it is about what *should* be, it is **contested**: there are scientific and political controversies about what sustainability means in practice.

This has two consequences for people who work with the concept:

- **Reflectivity** – you have to think about your own values and the values behind a policy or a school decision.
- **Negotiation and societal learning** – the transition towards sustainability relies on constant negotiation between different goals and on learning together (UNESCO 2022).

That is why the term "**(great) transformation**" became central: sustainability is not one reform, but a long process of societal change (WBGU 2011).

Because different people and groups weigh values differently, the concept is **contested** and needs reflection and negotiation. It is not a fixed measurement, and it is not a single legal rule – it is a guideline societies have to work out together.

## 3 Justice: the normative core

Where does the "should" come from? At the core of the normative idea are two forms of justice:

- **Intergenerational justice** – responsibility for **future generations**: they must also be able to satisfy their needs.
- **Intragenerational justice** – responsibility for different **people living today**: all humans should be able to enjoy a decent life that at least satisfies their fundamental needs.

"Inter" means *between*, "intra" means *within*. So: between generations – and within the generations that are alive now.

**Intergenerational justice** looks across time: future generations must also be able to satisfy their needs. **Intragenerational justice** looks across people: everyone living today should be able to enjoy a decent life that at least satisfies fundamental needs – not only people in low-income countries. The two forms are closely linked: decisions we make today about resources and education affect both people today *and* people tomorrow.

## 4 Two readings: weak vs. strong sustainability

There is one important debate inside the concept. It is about **natural capital** – forests, clean air, soil, biodiversity. Can it be replaced by human-made capital such as technology and infrastructure?

- **Weak sustainability:** yes – natural capital can be **substituted** by human-made capital. The focus is on **efficiency**: do more with less.
- **Strong sustainability:** no – natural capital is **non-substitutable** and must be preserved, especially critical ecosystems and biodiversity. The focus is on **sufficiency**: use less, and protect what cannot be replaced.

**Watch for:** what "substitution" of natural capital means, and what the speaker says about its limits.

## 5 Another lens: sustainable livelihoods

Not everyone starts from the global economy. There is an alternative view shaped by **women's movements in developing countries**: **sustainable livelihoods**.

It focuses on local conditions of life, livelihood security and the everyday experiences of women. A **livelihood** is "the capabilities, assets (including both material and social resources) and activities required for a means of living". In many of the regions where this view developed, the **subsistence economy** – producing for your own household first – is of major importance.

Why does this matter for you? Because a definition that starts from global growth and capital can miss what a farming family, a small workshop or a rural community actually needs to live. The livelihoods view keeps those everyday realities in the picture.

> **Learning journal:** Pick two of the definitions from page 1 (for example Brundtland and sustainable livelihoods). What do they share, and where do they differ? Write 4–6 sentences.

<details>
<summary>Compare with an expert view</summary>

Most definitions share the idea that **people's needs must be met** and that this should last – "forever" in the African paraphrase, "future generations" in Brundtland. The differences are in the starting point. Brundtland starts from **development** and the global economy; sustainable livelihoods starts from **local life** and the everyday capabilities and assets of people, especially women. Brundtland is a guideline for governments and the economy; the livelihoods view is a reminder that sustainability must work in a household and a village first. A good comparison names one shared core (needs, time) and one difference in scope (global economy vs. local life).

</details>

## 6 Scenario: a "carbon neutral" promise

You are a **ministry officer** in the Ministry of Education. A company offers to fully fund a new workshop in a VET school – on one condition: the school must present the workshop as **"carbon neutral"**. The company's plan: the workshop will still run as usual, and the company will buy carbon credits and plant trees to compensate for the emissions.

Think about the two readings you just learned. Which one does this plan rely on?

**Check:** Which interpretation of sustainability does the company's plan rely on?

- [(X)] Weak sustainability
- [( )] Strong sustainability
- [( )] Neither – it is not about sustainability at all
[[?]] The company does not reduce the emissions at the source. What does it rely on instead?
****************************************
This is a classic **weak sustainability** move – it is the same pattern as carbon offsetting by airlines: **emissions continue**, and the company relies on **tree planting or credits instead of reduction**. Natural capital is treated as something that can be substituted. A **strong sustainability** reading would ask first how much can be reduced at the source – for example by changing the energy supply of the workshop – and would protect, not replace, the ecosystems that absorb carbon.
****************************************

> **Learning journal:** In this scenario, what two questions would you ask the company before accepting the funding?

<details>
<summary>Compare with an expert view</summary>

A ministry officer would want to keep the money and test the promise. Two strong questions: **How much will the school actually reduce at the source** (energy, materials, transport) – because offsets only work if reduction comes first? And **what exactly do the credits and tree planting replace** – is the company assuming that planting trees can fully substitute the emissions of a running workshop? A school developer might ask who verifies the claims, and a principal would ask what the workshop's real energy use will be. The key point: accepting the label "carbon neutral" without reduction is accepting the weak-sustainability assumption of substitution.

</details>

## What this means in Serbia

Sustainability is not only a textbook concept – in Serbia it already shows up as concrete normative choices:

- Serbia's **Strategy for Low Carbon Development 2023–2030** includes a 2050 vision with **green jobs**. That is a normative choice about what kind of economy Serbia wants to build.
- The 2022 update of Serbia's **Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC)** – Serbia's own climate target under the global climate agreement – commits to cutting greenhouse gas emissions by **33.3 % by 2030 compared with 1990** (excluding land use). That is a political decision that weighs economic, social and ecological goals against each other – exactly the negotiation the concept demands.
