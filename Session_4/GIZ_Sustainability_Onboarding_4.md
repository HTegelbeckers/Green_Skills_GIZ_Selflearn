## Why this topic matters

So far, sustainability may have felt like an environmental topic. But it has a strong social side, too. In the **three-pillar model**, sustainability rests on three pillars: ecological, social and economic. In the **doughnut model**, the inner ring is a **social foundation**: the minimum every person needs – for example food, health, education, and work.

At the heart of the social dimension is **justice**. Researchers distinguish two forms (Michelsen et al. 2016):

- **Intragenerational justice** – fairness towards people living today: everyone should be able to lead a decent life that at least satisfies fundamental needs.
- **Intergenerational justice** – fairness towards future generations.

This matters for the green transition, too. The shift to a green economy must be **fair** for workers and regions: this is called a **just transition**. The EU's Just Transition Mechanism supports the regions and workers most affected by the shift.

This is also why your work matters. UNESCO (2022) says that vocational education and training (TVET) should help develop skills for **inclusive** and sustainable economies and for **inclusive and peaceful societies**.

Watch this short video from the International Labour Organization (ILO). Watch for: why workers and communities must be part of the transition, and what "people and planet first" means in practice.

## Learning journal

> **Learning journal:** Name one social issue in your region that has a link to the green transition – for example a change in jobs, a health or food concern, or a group that is affected more than others. Write 3–5 sentences: what is the issue, and who in your system (ministry, school, or company) is involved?

<details>
<summary>Compare with an expert view</summary>

There is no single correct answer – but a good reflection names a **concrete group** and a **concrete link** to the transition. A **ministry officer** might point to regions whose industry is shrinking and ask how qualifications and funding follow the people. A **school developer** might look at which occupations students in their region will actually have in ten years, and whether training places reach the students who need them most. A **principal** might notice who is most affected when the campus changes – for example staff with low incomes or students without easy access to transport. All three views share the same core question of a just transition: who benefits, who is affected, and who has a say?

</details>
