## The Roots of the Word

    --{{0}}--
The modern German word Nachhaltigkeit goes back to the noun Nachhalt. It describes an old survival strategy. Do not use everything today. Keep something for a hard winter.

<div style="display:flex;gap:1.5rem;flex-wrap:wrap;align-items:center;">
<div style="flex:1 1 260px;">

![Title page of Sylvicultura oeconomica, 1713](img/Carlowitz_Sylvicultura.jpg "Sylvicultura oeconomica, title page – Hans Carl von Carlowitz (1713) – public domain – via Wikimedia Commons")

</div>
<div style="flex:2 1 320px;">

The modern German word *Nachhaltigkeit* goes back to the noun *Nachhalt*. It originally meant "support, backing" or "what is held back": reserves kept for times of scarcity. It describes an old survival strategy of **precaution and self-limitation**.

The adjective *nachhaltig* / *nachhalten* is documented in German by the end of the 18th century. [CHECK: Juliana – confirm the dating; Carlowitz used "nachaltende" already in 1713]

</div>
</div>

    --{{1}}--
This explains a lot.

      {{1}}
> [!NOTE] Key message
> At its root, sustainability is about **not using everything at once**.

## Carlowitz and the Wood Crisis

    --{{0}}--
Now to the man himself. In the eighteenth century, German-speaking territories faced a severe wood shortage. In 1713, Carlowitz published the first systematic description of sustainable forestry.

<div style="display:flex;gap:1.5rem;flex-wrap:wrap;align-items:center;">
<div style="flex:1 1 260px;">

![Portrait of Hans Carl von Carlowitz, engraving](img/Hans_Carl_von_Carlowitz.jpg "Hans Carl von Carlowitz – Martin Bernigeroth (engraving, 18th century) – public domain")

</div>
<div style="flex:2 1 320px;">

In the 18th century, German-speaking territories faced a severe **wood shortage**. Forests were over-used for timber trade, war, mining and smelting, tanning and glass-making. Forests shrank, and erosion and soil degradation threatened whole regions.

**Hans Carl von Carlowitz (1645–1714)** was the Saxon chief mining official (*Oberberghauptmann*) in Freiberg. In **1713** he published *Sylvicultura oeconomica*. He called for a "continuous, steady and *nachaltende* (sustained) use" of forests.

He also argued **economically**: a country that destroys its forests destroys the basis of its own economy. For him, sustainability was a smart long-term strategy, not altruism.

</div>
</div>

    --{{1}}--
His core rule was simple.

      {{1}}
> [!NOTE] Key message
> **Cut only as much wood as can grow back** through reforestation.

## Four Core Components

    --{{0}}--
Carlowitz' idea was developed further by Georg Ludwig Hartig, in his work of 1804. Hartig wrote that the present generation should use the forest as much as possible, but so that the descendants can draw at least as much advantage from it. It contains four core components.

**What does forest sustainability consist of?**

    --{{1}}--
First, long-term thinking.

      {{1}}
- **Long-term thinking:** the forest's services (timber, water storage, erosion protection, habitat) must be secured continuously; planning over decades to centuries

    --{{2}}--
Second, social obligation.

      {{2}}
- **Social obligation:** forests also serve public functions; public interests can limit private property rights

    --{{3}}--
Third, economy.

      {{3}}
- **Economy:** sustainability does not mean "no use", but economic use with planned protection of the resource

    --{{4}}--
And fourth, responsibility.

      {{4}}
- **Responsibility:** awareness of the forest's importance for future generations

    --{{5}}--
And that is an early form of a very modern idea.

      {{5}}
> [!TIP] Why this matters
> **Hartig (1764–1837)** wrote in his work of 1804 that the present generation should use the forest, but so "that the descendants can draw at least as much advantage from it". This is an early form of **intergenerational justice**: fairness towards future generations.

## Live from the Interest

    --{{0}}--
The forest idea is often explained with a money metaphor. A forest is managed sustainably if the annual harvest equals the annual growth. This is also how Milica looks at her budget.

<!-- data-type="none" -->
| Criterion | Interest | Capital |
|-----------|---------------|---------------|
| In a forest | the annual growth | the stock of trees |
| Sustainable use | may be used | stays intact |
| In a school workshop | the value the workshop produces year after year | the working capacity: machines, trained staff, the budget line for maintenance |
| Warning sign | the harvest of a year is larger than the growth | the stock is shrinking |

    --{{1}}--
Here are two examples.

      {{1}}
- **Interest:** The wood that regrows in one year
- **Capital:** The pipeline of qualified teachers, which must not shrink below what schools need

    --{{2}}--
There is one error to avoid.

      {{2}}
> [!WARNING] Common misconception
> "Sustainability means no use." Carlowitz' rule was to use the resource, but not to destroy the stock that produces it. Profit maximisation, on the other hand, is the opposite of the idea.

    --{{3}}--
Now you sort them.

      {{3}}
